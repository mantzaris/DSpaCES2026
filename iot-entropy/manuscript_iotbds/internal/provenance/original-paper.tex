\begin{abstract}
A faulty sensor may begin copying its neighbors while producing apparently plausible readings. Detecting such changes requires examining both relationships between sensors and patterns within individual streams. We investigate spatial and temporal entropy as complementary measurements for IoT fault detection through a GPU implementation that compares conventional statistics with entropy features under historical resampling and generative diffusion references. Experiments cover 360 fault realizations across 20 simulations and recording blocks, using one synthetic dataset and two real datasets with injected faults. Temporal entropy and autocorrelation capture changes the spatial covariance can ignore. On Intel data, temporal entropy with resampling achieves recall 0.389 versus 0.000 for conventional temporal statistics, with respective background alarm rates of 0.103 and 0.000. Missing data analysis helps explain observed performance differences. The findings set complementary measurement capabilities and provide practical guidance to evaluating entropy and generative models in sensor networks, with the benefits depending on fault mechanism, measurement availability and calibration.
\end{abstract}
\begin{IEEEkeywords}
IoT, spatial entropy, permutation entropy, sample entropy, diffusion forecasting, fault localization
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
Sensors in digital twins and data spaces need evidence about changes in both
collective organization and individual dynamics. Copying streams can reduce
independent spatial variation, replay can alter the temporal organization without
creating a large marginal outlier. A legitimate operating transition can also
change both. An alert should therefore expose measurements and their provenance,
rather than assert an unverified physical cause.

This study compares information captured by spatial and temporal entropy with
conventional measurements, separating the features from its normal reference
model. There is a motivating connection to prior work, that entropy
trajectories describe evolving homogeneous clusting under different network
topologies~\cite{mantzaris2024} and collective flocking
organization~\cite{mantzaris2025}. We transfer attention to evolving organization
and interaction structure. The segregation study uses degree/homophily
distributions, the flocking work develops a thermodynamic description of its
simulation. Here spatial entropy uses normalized correlation eigenvalues,
permutation entropy uses ordinal patterns, and sample entropy uses matching
templates. These definitions differ. We infer neither thermodynamic quantities
nor a second law for sensor recordings.

The earlier version of this project evaluated spatial correlation spectrum
entropy and established no consistent improvement over its synchronization
comparators. That finding motivates, but does not predetermine, this extension.
A changing spatial entropy trace is not itself a within sensor temporal entropy
estimator. Nor does an unsuccessful reference model establish a limitation of
all entropy features. The original artifacts remain identifiable, new
comparisons, support corrections and diagnostics have separate manifests.

We address six practical and theoretical questions. \textbf{RQ1}: does spatial entropy improve detection
or localization over non-entropic spatial statistics? \textbf{RQ2}: do temporal
entropies reveal changes missed by spatial covariance summaries?
\textbf{RQ3}: do they improve over conventional temporal statistics?
\textbf{RQ4}: does combining spatial and temporal evidence improve detection
or localization? \textbf{RQ5}: does a generative reference improve over joint
block bootstrap? \textbf{RQ6}: how do support, missingness, window length,
calibration and mechanism affect these answers?

Our contribution is a reproducible comparison, elementary theoretical
characterization of the information retained by its measurements, and an
inspectable monitoring artifact. We introduce neither an entropy definition
nor a general purpose diffusion architecture. Conventional features augmented
with entropy are compared directly with conventional features alone; entropy
is not required to win or lose.

\section{Related Work}
Effective rank measures spectral dimensionality through entropy of normalized
singular values~\cite{effective_rank}, these equal eigenvalues for a PSD
correlation matrix. Graph entropy anomaly monitoring also predates this
study~\cite{finger}, although a graph Laplacian spectrum differs from the
correlation spectrum used here. Spatial meaning comes from physical/proximity
groups, not from the ordering of sensor columns.

Bandt and Pompe's permutation entropy summarizes neighboring ordinal
patterns~\cite{bandt2002}. Richman and Moorman's sample entropy describes
template-extension regularity~\cite{richman2000}. Their probability objects,
support requirements and amplitude sensitivity differ. Costa et al's
multiscale sample entropy~\cite{costa2002} explicitly warns against identifying
irregularity with healthy complexity. We separately label coarse-grained
permutation and sample entropy and make no physiological health inference.

TimeGrad~\cite{timegrad}, CSDI~\cite{csdi} and DiffSTG~\cite{diffstg} establish
diffusion forecasting, conditional imputation and graph-conditioned generation.
DiffAD~\cite{diffad}, ImDiffusion~\cite{imdiffusion} and
ICDiffAD~\cite{icdiffad} already apply diffusion to anomaly detection. Our
generator uses a forecasting boundary: it receives no measured target value.
GDN~\cite{gdn} supplies a graph-prediction comparator, with adaptation and
information access disclosed below. Data dependent conformal
work~\cite{conformal} motivates explicit calibration assumptions, we do not
claim a new distribution free guarantee for an indefinite sensor stream.




\section{Measurements, References and Decisions}



\subsection{Groups, channels and spatial measurements}
We examine sensor groups to measure how their readings change together.
Groups contain $m\in\{6,12,24\}$ sensors around 24 centers selected at
regular intervals in node index order. Sensors are selected by graph
distance, measured in hops, with geographic distance resolving ties.
If a connected component contains too few sensors, additional nodes are
selected by node order. Duplicate groups of the same size are removed.
The graphs describe coupling in the synthetic system, coordinate proximity
in Intel data, and the supplied road distances in PEMS. Each measurement
channel is analyzed separately. The main analysis uses windows of
$W\in\{48,96\}$ observations and compares measurements $d=W/4$ steps
apart. The original $W=24$ analysis is retained separately.

For each group and channel, we use only timestamps at which every sensor
has an observation. The number of usable rows must satisfy
$n\geq\max(2m,\lceil.8W\rceil)$.
If any sensor has sample variance at most $10^{-8}$ in units scaled
using the training data, the correlation calculation is marked undefined
and flagged for data quality. Otherwise, we center each sensor column
and scale it to unit sample variance, producing the matrix $Z$.
We then calculate
\begin{align}
\R_{g,t}&=Z^\top Z/(n-1),\\
\widetilde\R_{g,t}&=(1-\lambda)\R_{g,t}+\lambda I,\\
p_k&=\lambda_k(\widetilde\R_{g,t})/\operatorname{tr}(\widetilde\R_{g,t}),\\[-2mm]
H_S(g,t)&=-\sum_k p_k\log p_k/\log m,\\
\Delta H_S(g,t)&=H_S(g,t)-H_S(g,t-d).
\label{eq:spatial}
\end{align}
Here $\R_{g,t}$ is the correlation matrix for group $g$ at time $t$.
The identity contribution, with $\lambda=.05$, stabilizes the matrix,
and $p_k$ gives the proportion of its trace associated with eigenvalue $k$.
Entropy is lower when variation is concentrated in a few shared patterns
and higher when it is distributed more evenly across patterns.
The signed change $\Delta H_S$ records both increases and decreases.
This is \emph{local correlation-spectrum entropy}, with spatial meaning
provided by the sensor groups. We use $0\log0=0$.
The symmetric eigensolver rejects negative eigenvalues beyond the tolerance value
of $64m$ times machine precision. TF32 is disabled when computing
$Z^\top Z$.

Conventional spatial measurements include mean signed correlation $C$,
mean absolute correlation, standardized pairwise disagreement $D$,
the leading eigenvalue concentration $\lambda_{\max}(\R)/m$, and
their changes over the same interval. For the standardized columns,
$D=2(n-1)(1-C)/n$. Thus, when the number of usable rows is fixed,
$D$ contains the same information as $C$.
We also compare the complete correlation matrix $\R$ and its change
$\Delta\R$ against the reference trajectories using standardized
Frobenius discrepancies. These comparisons capture changes across all
sensor pairs. Entropy compresses this matrix into one value and cannot
add information to it, although the summary can behave differently
during estimation and calibration.




\subsection{Temporal entropy and data requirements}
We calculate each sensor's entropy before aggregation. For sensor $i$,
permutation entropy measures the diversity of orderings in
$v_{i,s}=(x_{i,s+j\tau})_{j=0}^{q-1}$. Window frequencies $p_{i,t}(\pi)$ give
\begin{equation}
 H_{\rm PE}(i,t)=-\sum_\pi p_{i,t}(\pi)\log p_{i,t}(\pi)/\log(q!).
 \label{eq:pe}
\end{equation}
Development compares $(q,\tau)=(3,1),(3,2),(4,1)$, starting from $(3,1)$.
Missing observations invalidate affected templates without closing time gaps.
Equal values are ordered by timestamp. Both windows require
$\max(30,5q!)$ valid templates. Flatlines and tied fractions above .5 are
excluded from scoring. We record tied fractions and test excluding tied
templates during development. For untied values, strictly monotone amplitude
transformations preserve entropy. Higher entropy need not indicate healthier
behavior.

Sample entropy measures how often similar sequences remain similar after
another observation. Using complete $(r+1)$-templates with $r=2$,
we form pairs $\mathcal P$ with starts $a<b$ and $b-a>2$, excluding
self matches and the Theiler neighborhood. Let $D_r$ denote the
maximum absolute difference across the first $r$ coordinates, with
$D_{r+1}$ including the next observation. Then
\begin{align}
B_r&=\sum_{(a,b)\in\mathcal P}\mathbf1\{D_r(a,b)\leq\delta\},\\
 A_{r+1}&=\sum_{(a,b)\in\mathcal P}\mathbf1\{D_{r+1}(a,b)\leq\delta\},\\
 H_{\rm SE}(i,t)&=-\log(A_{r+1}/B_r).\label{eq:se}
\end{align}
Both counts use identical pairs. Tolerance candidates .1, .2 and .3 multiply
the fixed training scale. Development maximizes the fraction of finite
estimates, with predefined tie preferences. $B_r=0$ gives an undefined
estimate, while $A_{r+1}=0<B_r$ gives a censored infinite value.
Scoring requires 30 templates, $B_r\geq20$, $A_{r+1}>0$, nonconstant
data and valid comparison windows. No pseudocounts or finite substitutes
are used. Match, pair and template counts remain visible alongside censoring.

Both entropies provide a level and lagged change. Multiscale diagnostics
average nonoverlapping bins of 1, 2 or 4 observations from the window's
left boundary. Bins with missing observations are invalid. Incomplete
final bins are discarded. Template requirements and forecast horizon
remain unchanged. Conventional comparators use identical histories.
A separately frozen exploratory comparison evaluates scales 1 and 2 on
$W=96$, retaining $d=24$ original time steps, 17 episodes and references.
Scale 2 floors are refitted using the original four development issuances,
and each family is recalibrated. Other settings remain fixed. Permutation,
autocorrelation and Theiler lags follow the coarse grid, doubling their
physical delays. Spatial measurements use the original grid.




\subsection{Conventional temporal measurements and feature families}
Temporal baselines measure persistence through autocorrelation (ACF) at lags
1, 2 and 4 and changes in this vector. Variability is measured by first difference
variance and local sample variance, drift by a least squares trend, and mean
departures by predictive deviation and a two-sided CUSUM. Lag pairs preserve
actual time positions. Each statistic requires at least
$\max(12,\lceil W/2\rceil)$ observations or valid pairs. CUSUM resets at each
window, uses allowance .5 in training-scaled units, pauses on missing rows,
and reports its final state. Reference scoring expresses measurements as
departures from expected behavior.

Additional detectors use raw predictive residuals and the retained GDN
adaptation. Raw residuals average absolute pointwise deviations divided by
(generated MAD plus .05), retaining the original spatial quality restriction.
GDN uses 32-dimensional embeddings, learned top-15 neighborhoods, graph
attention and a shared two-layer head for multiple channels without batch
normalization. Errors standardized on development data are averaged within
four trailing one step predictions before taking the group maximum. GDN receives
causal observed target history, giving it more recent information than the
long-lead references. Its group support and participation mapping are
adaptations, so published GDN benchmark accuracy is not claimed.
Feature families comprise spatial entropy (S), permutation entropy (PE),
sample entropy (SE), both temporal entropies (T), and all entropies (ST).
Conventional families are spatial (SB), temporal (TB), and combined (B).
BST adds all entropies to B. Families use the maximum component score.
Unsupported components are skipped, with abstention if none qualify.
Level/change pairs require both endpoints. Comparing B with BST tests
whether entropy improves conventional detectors.









\subsection{Joint bootstrap and generative references}
Both references generate possible future readings jointly across sensors,
using 48 observations to predict 120 steps. Inputs include calendar cycles,
coordinates and graph connections, but exclude future readings. Forecasts
for decision $t$ are issued at $t-120$. Current and earlier windows use
steps 25 to 120 and 1 to 96 for $W=96$, or 73 to 120 and 61 to 108 for $W=48$.
Both windows share each generated trajectory. This means forecasting
240 minutes ahead for Intel and 600 for PEMS.

Bootstrap resamples intact blocks from the 64 training donors most similar
in preceding readings and calendar position. The diffusion model is inspired
by DiffSTG/CSDI, using width 24, three graph-temporal blocks, historical
readings and missingness indicators, and fixed graph/calendar encodings.
Training adds Gaussian noise $\epsilon$ to trajectories $Y$ and learns to
predict that noise
\begin{align}
Y_s&=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_s}Y+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_s}\epsilon,\\
\mathcal L_{\rm diff}&=\E\left[
\frac{\|M_{\rm loss}\odot(\epsilon-\epsilon_\theta(Y_s,s,C,G))\|_F^2}
{\sum M_{\rm loss}}\right].\label{eq:diff}
\end{align}
Here $s$ indexes diffusion steps and $\bar\alpha_s$ controls retained signal.
The network $\epsilon_\theta$ uses context $C$ and graph $G$ to predict noise.
The mask $M_{\rm loss}$ restricts training error to observed targets.
Training uses a cosine schedule~\cite{cosine}, 100 diffusion steps, Adam
at $10^{-3}$, batches of 8, up to 2400 updates and development early stopping.
Generation starts from noise using 20 step DDIM~\cite{ddim}, clipping denoised
states to $[-8,8]$ training units. Seeds 17/29/43 are retained without
architecture tuning on inspected tests.
Each method generates 64 trajectories. Observed missingness and additional
dropout are applied after generation. Bootstrap donors retain further gaps.
Even for identical evaluation windows, usable row and template counts
can therefore differ.






\subsection{Scores, group aggregation and calibration}
For feature $a$, set $u_a=(f_a, f_a-f_{a,t-d})$. Componentwise generated medians
$c_{a,t,j}$ and MADs give
\begin{align}
z_{a,t,j}&=\frac{u_{a,t,j}-c_{a,t,j}}
{1.4826\,\operatorname{median}_b|u^{(b)}_{a,t,j}-c_{a,t,j}|+\epsilon_j},\\
A_a(t)&=\max_j|z_{a,t,j}|.\label{eq:score}
\end{align}
Floors for level/change features are $\max(10^{-4},.05\,\mathrm{IQR}_{dev})$.
Legacy matrix distance and raw residual comparators retain .01 and .05
regularization floors, respectively. Reference support counts are retained,
the temporal rule requires at least 52 finite draws, while the retained spatial
summary implementation uses 51 of 64. MAD scaling does not imply Gaussian tails.
Actual $\Delta f$ remains separate from $\Delta f-c_{\Delta f}$.

For each temporal group, average its largest $k=\lceil\eta m\rceil$ valid sensor
scores, requiring at least $\lceil m/2\rceil$ valid members. Fractions
$\eta\in\{.25,.5\}$ and localization budgets in $\{6,12,24\}$ are selected
jointly from four development copy/noise/drift/delay episodes. Mean localization
IoU is pooled over T, TB, ST, B and BST and both references. All families use
the same selected fraction and budget. Max combined families receive their
own calibration, no uncalibrated union of separately thresholded alerts is used.

With frozen family $\mathcal F$, let $M_t=\max_{a\in\mathcal F}A_a(t)$.
Calibration units are separated by 168 rows (48 context plus 120 target), or
are separate synthetic simulations. For $n_c$ calibration maxima,
\begin{equation}
p_t=\frac{1+\sum_{b=1}^{n_c}\mathbf1\{M_b\geq M_t\}}{n_c+1}.
\label{eq:rank}
\end{equation}
An entirely abstained unit has $M=-\infty$ and cannot alert. Primary
$\alpha=.10$ is fixed before testing. Temporal dependence and distribution
shift can invalidate exchangeability despite gaps; this is a per-issuance
statement, not a stream-wide guarantee. Neither $1-p$ nor a model deviation
is a physical fault probability. These test controls evaluate background rates;
separate retrospective rate caps are clearly descriptive, not the prospective
threshold validation.









\subsection{Localization and availability}
Group participation weights each group's score by inverse size and normalizes
over eligible incident groups. Direct temporal ranking uses sensor scores,
the combined rule averages their normalized rank percentiles. S/SB use
participation, T/PE/SE/TB direct ranking, and ST/B/BST the combined rule.
All three alternatives are also reported. The budget is selected on development
data, never from test truth. Candidate group best overlap IoU is an oracle
diagnostic, not a deployable method. Participation is not causal attribution.

Operational analysis includes every scheduled unit and injected event, including
abstentions and misses. A separately calibrated common support analysis uses
group/window/channel units valid for S, PE, SE, SB and TB within each reference.
Reference comparisons also report their differing model support counts, common
feature support is not claimed to eliminate every reference support difference.
Conditional localization is reported only alongside unconditional scores, where
misses contribute zero. Quality flags, signed agreement and onset alignment
are descriptive and do not create additional uncalibrated detections.





\section{Theoretical and Numerical Characterization}

\begin{proposition}[Bounds and shrinkage]
For a PSD correlation matrix, $0\leq H_S\leq1$. At $\lambda=0$, identity
has entropy one and rank one entropy zero. With positive shrinkage the lower
bound is $-[a\log a+(m-1)b\log b]/\log m$, where
$a=1-\lambda+\lambda/m$ and $b=\lambda/m$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The eigenvalues normalized by trace form a probability vector. Shannon
entropy is nonnegative and maximized by uniform probabilities. Shrinkage maps
its simplex into the convex hull of permutations of $(a,b,\ldots,b)$;
concavity makes these extreme points minimizers.\end{proof}

For equicorrelation $\R(\rho)=(1-\rho)I+\rho\mathbf1\mathbf1^\top$,
\begin{equation}
\frac{dH_S}{d\rho}=\frac{m-1}{m\log m}
\log\frac{1-\rho}{1+(m-1)\rho}\leq0,\quad 0\leq\rho<1.
\end{equation}
The eigenvalues are $1+(m-1)\rho$ and $1-\rho$ with multiplicity $m-1$.
Substitution proves the derivative; shrinkage replaces $\rho$ by
$(1-\lambda)\rho$ and multiplies by $1-\lambda$. The displayed nonpositive
sign is restricted to nonnegative $\rho$, not the whole PSD interval.

Entropy need not be determined by average correlation. For $m=4$, compare
equicorrelation .2 with two independent two-sensor blocks of correlation .6.
Both have $C=C_{abs}=.2$ and leading concentration .4, but unshrunk entropies
are .960964 and .860964. Their full matrices distinguish them. This is an
analytic example, not empirical evidence of detector superiority.

\begin{proposition}[Order invariance on an aligned complete window]
For a common time-row permutation $P$, the correlation matrix and its
correlation-spectrum entropy are unchanged.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Column centering and sample scaling commute with row permutation, and
$(PZ)^\top(PZ)/(n-1)=Z^\top P^\top PZ/(n-1)=Z^\top Z/(n-1)$.
\end{proof}
Order-sensitive temporal measurements can change. This elementary property
applied to measurement design is not a new theorem. It does not imply invariance
of every overlapping rolling window or the whole entropy-change trace. Offsets,
nonzero column gains and sign conjugation also leave the correlation spectrum
unchanged; temporal ordinal entropy can likewise miss amplitude changes.

\begin{proposition}[Conditional rank calibration]
Conditional on a fixed scoring rule, if calibration and next-null maxima are
exchangeable, (\ref{eq:rank}) is super-uniform. Its smallest attainable value
is $1/(n_c+1)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Among $n_c+1$ exchangeable scores the test score's upper rank is uniform in
the absence of ties; including all tied calibration scores makes it
conservative. The trained rule must be fixed before these units.\end{proof}
The premise is not established merely by chronological partitioning. Controlled
null simulations, finite-difference derivatives, explicit CPU pair counts,
CPU/GPU agreement and leakage/event-matching checks accompany the proof.

\section{Experimental Design and Evaluation}
We test whether the entropy estimation complements conventional measurements and if
generative references improve fault detection. We use one synthetic dataset
and two real sensor datasets with injected faults. Synthetic networks provide
controlled dynamics, while real recordings introduce practical variability
and missing data. Three synthetic network sizes give five configurations,
with 360 fault realizations across 20 background blocks and three model
seeds. The protocol was fixed before full runs. Earlier work inspected
the real backgrounds, making those comparisons exploratory.

\textbf{Synthetic data.} Random geometric graphs contain 64, 128 or 256
sensors. Their readings evolve according to
$x_{t+1}=(.82I-.25L/\max_i\deg_i)x_t+Bf_t+.12\epsilon_t$,
where $L$ is the graph Laplacian, $Bf_t$ represents three shared
Ornstein Uhlenbeck drivers with spatial loadings, and $\epsilon_t$ supplies
random noise. Calendar signals add periodic variation. The transition matrix
has spectral radius .82, and 256 initial steps are discarded.
Training data and scaling remain fixed. Independent seeds generate four
development simulations of 312 observations, 32 calibration simulations of
256 observations, and four test simulations of 312 observations per size.
Windows remain within individual simulations. Display timestamps represent
simulation phase.

\textbf{Intel Lab.} This dataset~\cite{intel} contains readings from 54 sensors
approximately every 31 seconds. Measurements include temperature, humidity,
light and voltage. We analyze temperature and humidity, retaining the other
channels for auxiliary analysis. Each 2 minute interval contributes its
last available reading at the interval endpoint, preserving causal timing
and missingness. Groups follow sensor coordinates. A fixed coverage rule
excludes data from March 24, 2004 onward, leaving 17,970 observations on the
time grid. Chronological training, development, calibration and test
partitions contain 60/15/10/15\% of these observations.

\textbf{PEMS-BAY.} The DCRNN release~\cite{dcrnn} records traffic speed in mph
from 325 road sensors every five minutes during January to June 2017.
Preprocessing inserts 12 missing timestamps, masks 521 zero readings under
the release convention, and retains published road distances. The resulting
52,128 timestamps use the same chronological partition proportions as Intel.
Records identifying individual values imputed upstream are unavailable.
Although selected blocks excluded the earlier primary experiments, a broader
diagnostic had inspected them. Both real datasets therefore provide previously
examined backgrounds with injected faults. Actual physical faults are unverified.

\textbf{Preprocessing and calibration.} Measurements are centered using
training medians and scaled by IQR/1.349, with documented fallbacks.
Missing neural inputs use zero plus a mask, while statistical estimators
retain missing values. Missing values are never filled from neighbors.
Windows stay within partitions. Models and groups remain fixed.
Thresholds are fitted without test labels, using the previously examined real
calibration partitions. There are 32 synthetic, 10 Intel and 31 PEMS forecast
issuances for calibration. Their minimum rank p-values are .0303, .0909 and
.03125. Intel's ten calibration cases therefore permit only coarse threshold
choices near nominal .10.

\textbf{Fault scenarios.} Twelve mechanisms cover copying, common interference,
decoupling, independent noise, delay, offset/gain drift, flatline, dropout,
anticorrelated copying, mixed collapse/noise, covariance changes preserving
average correlation, and temporal rearrangements preserving marginal values.
Combining severities .6/1 with durations 12/48/96 gives 72 fault realizations
per configuration. Affected groups contain 6 or 12 sensors and may be connected
or partly disconnected. Synthetic decoupling changes the state transition
while sharing randomness with its clean counterpart. Real decoupling modifies
recordings without changing the physical system. Faults begin at observation
192. Decisions occur at 167,179,$\ldots$,311, placing the first possible
detection at 203, eleven samples after onset. Forecast inputs precede every
fault. Unmodified backgrounds and legitimate synchronized transitions provide
controls. Faults constitute 90\% of these constructed trials, so precision
reflects this benchmark's event mix rather than deployment prevalence.

\textbf{Alert evaluation.} Consecutive flagged decisions form one alert.
Hungarian matching pairs alerts and events one to one, crediting only alerts
beginning during an event. Event boundaries are not extended, and point
labels are not adjusted after detection. Unmatched early, late and control
alerts enter benchmark precision. We report alarms on unlabelled real
backgrounds as background alerts. Event precision-recall area uses the upper
precision  over recalls because changing thresholds can
merge alerts and make recall nonmonotonically. Detection delay includes the
decision schedule. Computation time is measured as a different source.

\textbf{Uncertainty and comparison controls.} Paired percentile intervals
resample complete simulations or recording blocks, keeping their repeated
injections and three model seeds together. Overlapping windows do not count
as independent repetitions. Four blocks per configuration limit precision,
and adjacent recording blocks may remain dependent. Intervals are exploratory,
without multiple comparison adjustment or a planned equivalence test.
Results are separated by mechanism, severity, duration, measured change
direction and data availability. All feature comparisons fix the physical
or proximity graph. Level/change pairs require valid endpoints, and matrix
comparisons include both $\R$ and $\Delta\R$. This keeps scoring rules
consistent across entropy and conventional features.




\section{Results}

\subsection{Fault detection, localization and added value}
Table~\ref{tab:main} and Fig.~\ref{fig:performance} distinguish detecting
faults, measured by recall, from identifying affected sensors, measured by IoU.
Background rates describe the alarm burden on data without injected faults.
For spatial measurements (RQ1), detection gains often accompany more
background alarms. Synthetic diffusion S minus SB gives recall
$-0.099\;[-0.174,-0.034]$ and IoU $-0.010\;[-0.017,-0.003]$.
Intel diffusion S detects fewer faults than SB (0.213 versus 0.481),
with fewer background alarms (0.218 versus 0.372).
PEMS bootstrap S detects more faults (0.528 versus 0.005), but its
background rate is 0.301 versus 0.000. Capping background rates
retrospectively at .10 reverses this ordering, with recalls 0.069 versus
0.264 and rates 0.026 versus 0.064. This illustrates threshold sensitivity,
without validating future alarm control.

Temporal entropy (RQ3) helps in some settings. Synthetic bootstrap T/TB
recall is 0.401/0.532, a difference of $-0.131\;[-0.313,+0.054]$,
with IoU 0.093/0.239. Intel bootstrap T exceeds TB by
$+0.389\;[+0.264,+0.523]$ recall, at background rates 0.103 versus 0.000.
Sample entropy has higher Intel recall and sensor overlap than permutation
entropy, with recall 0.380 versus 0.310 and IoU 0.073 versus 0.022.
Diffusion reduces Intel T recall to 0.060 while increasing background
alarms to 0.288. PEMS temporal comparisons remain less specific.

Combining features (RQ4) does not automatically preserve their individual
strengths. Synthetic bootstrap ST improves recall over S by
$+0.123\;[+0.028,+0.230]$, but over T by only
$+0.008\;[-0.085,+0.127]$. Their localization rules differ.
Additional comparisons hold these rules fixed.
Adding entropy to conventional B increases synthetic bootstrap recall by
$+0.003\;[+0.000,+0.008]$ and IoU by $+0.008\;[+0.000,+0.017]$,
using identical localization rules. Recall remains unchanged in the other
five dataset/reference combinations. Conventional components usually dominate
the maximum score, and each family has its own calibrated threshold.
For Intel bootstrap, T detects faults while B and BST detect none at the
selected threshold. This does not establish feature equivalence.

Additional detectors offer different tradeoffs. GDN synthetic/Intel/PEMS
recall is 0.514/0.139/0.157, with background rates 0.135/0.436/0.038.
PEMS bootstrap residuals achieve recall 0.264 at background rate 0.077.
Their information access and support differ. Localization remains difficult.
For detected faults, bootstrap T IoU is 0.231/0.195/0.038 across the three

\subsection{Complementary responses to different fault mechanisms}
RQ2 shows how spatial and temporal measurements complement each other.
In 64 aligned simulations, rearranging time points identically across sensors
preserves their correlation matrix to within $1.3\times10^{-15}$ (rounded
upward). Mean PE rises from 0.964 to 0.989 and SE from 0.586 to 1.115,
while ACF1 falls from 0.845 to -0.016. Spatial entropy remains unchanged.
Copying lowers spatial entropy from 0.877 to 0.134, while PE changes
from 0.964 to 0.965. Additional diagnostics examine offsets, gains, sign
changes, quantization, replay, periodic interference, irregularity, legitimate
transitions and dropout. These diagnostics isolate responses separately
from the primary benchmark.

The synthetic benchmark demonstrates practical strengths. Bootstrap temporal
entropy detects 74.1\% of flatline events versus 24.1\% for conventional
temporal measurements. For independent noise, conventional recall is 1.000
versus 0.463 for temporal entropy. These responses occur at unequal achieved
background alarm rates. Windows spanning a flatline onset can retain
measurable entropy, while wholly constant windows trigger abstention.
Paired results distinguish mechanism, severity, duration, measured direction
and affected data availability. Direction denotes the observed lagged difference.
The first decision after onset occurs 11 samples later, highlighting how
window length and decision frequency influence detection of short events.

\section{Dashboard and GPU Computation}
The dashboard links alerts to sensor behavior. Operators can inspect
contributing sensors, coordinated changes and departures from generated
references. Fixed sensor positions support visual comparison, with separate
overlays for physical connections and correlation changes.

Selecting a group reveals individual entropy traces, conventional
measurements and predictive bands. The display separates actual increases
or decreases from departures relative to expected behavior. Template and
pair counts indicate whether enough observations support an estimate.
Shared channels, change direction and onset timing help users investigate
an alert without assigning a physical cause. Forecast and observation times,
model version and the scan calibrated p-value provide context.
Three prespecified copying examples demonstrate the workflow without
selecting for success.

CUDA batches correlation matrices, eigenvalues, ordinal patterns,
conventional statistics and joint trajectory generation. Sample entropy
runs in bounded batches to limit temporary memory. Statistical calculations
use float32 and are checked against CPU float64, separately from neural
mixed precision. Stored predictions, calibration results, missingness
information and reference trajectories support reconstruction of the
displayed evidence.

On an RTX A6000, median time from reference generation through sensor
localization is 0.180, 0.174 and 0.258 seconds for bootstrap on synthetic64,
Intel and PEMS, respectively. Diffusion takes 0.370, 0.338 and 1.164 seconds.
Peak allocated memory is at most 1.43 GiB.
These timings include missingness handling, both windows, feature
extraction, scoring and localization. They exclude GDN, data acquisition,
loading, disk output, rank lookup and dashboard rendering.

For PEMS, the temporal calculation takes 0.724 seconds on CPU float64 and
0.035 seconds on GPU float32, excluding transfers. The comparison combines
hardware and precision differences, while maximum numerical disagreement
remains below $2.0\times10^{-7}$. Timed GPU stages totaled 4.271 hours
across experiments, validation and interrupted attempts.
The core computation is shorter than the real datasets' sampling intervals.
Overall response time also includes data handling, window accumulation
and decision scheduling.

\section{Conclusion}
This study provides an integrated approach to evaluating the entropy for IoT
fault detections. The contribution is the joint assessment of measurement
information, data availability, generative reference quality and alarm
calibration, supported by GPU computation and an inspectable dashboard.
Spatial and temporal measurements capture different changes, supporting
feature selection according to sensor behavior and fault mechanisms.

Temporal entropy expands availability on incomplete Intel data and improves
detection under the evaluated bootstrap calibration, with unequal background
rates. Conventional temporal measurements provide stronger synthetic
localization. Adding entropy increases synthetic bootstrap recall by 0.003
and leaves other primary recalls unchanged, identifying limited incremental
benefit under the tested maximum rule. Neither entropy nor the evaluated
diffusion reference shows a consistent overall advantage.
A central finding is that better raw predictive scores need not produce
faithful entropy distributions. This gives generative monitoring a concrete
evaluation requirement and helps distinguish model deficiencies from
measurement limitations.

The evidence concerns injected faults and partly reused recordings, with
four blocks per real dataset. Forecast lead, calibration size, estimator
support and candidate groups shape the conclusions. The framework supports
validation on native faults and longer matched histories, together with
operator studies toward practical deployment.
